The new AI risk is dependence, not just cheating

Fresh research and this week's policy signals point to a sharper agenda for schools: protect student thinking, design AI around learning, and make assessment evidence harder to outsource.

1Top story
China · Research

A 1,029-student study maps two routes into AI dependence

A new study of 1,029 Chinese high school and university students argues that generative AI dependence is not one behavior but two pathways: deep cognitive absorption and a more utilitarian habit of using AI because it reduces effort. The research, summarized by BIOENGINEER, applies the I-PACE behavioral-addiction model to student AI use.

That matters for schools because the remedies differ. A student mesmerized by chatbot interaction may need attention-management routines; a student using AI as an academic shortcut may need tasks that rebuild the value of independent effort.

The next integrity problem is not only whether AI wrote the answer. It is whether students are losing the habit of doing the thinking before the tool arrives.
2Research & evidence

Evidence is converging on cognitive work

Psychological literacy joins AI literacy
The student-dependence study links overreliance to cognitive absorption and perceived usefulness, suggesting AI guidance should teach learners how their own attention and motivation respond to tools. BIOENGINEER.
Educators feel ready, but training is uneven
An IBM/Morning Consult survey reported by Education Week found 83% of educators confident teaching about AI, while many still learn through social media, friends, or family rather than formal professional development. Education Week.
Learning science warns against cognitive offloading
Microsoft's education principles emphasize AI that supports learning rather than replacing student thinking, with age-appropriate controls and scaffolded practice. Microsoft.
